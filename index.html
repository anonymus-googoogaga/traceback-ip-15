01 — The problem

The real question is not "where did it go?"

It is: how can a rural victim create enough evidence to identify a vehicle after it has already escaped?

Accidentin a village road, often after dark
→
Vehicle escapestakes a connecting road, no camera
→
Victim reportsFIR, maybe an application
→
Vehicle unidentifiednow a dented car at a garage

What the victim actually knows

  • Approximate vehicle colour
  • Vehicle type — car, two-wheeler, tractor, tempo
  • Direction of travel
  • Rough time, and the location
  • Maybe a partial registration number
  • Sometimes a photograph, sometimes a witness

That is the entire evidence budget. It is small, but it is not zero — if you can find a place where this vehicle must show up.

What happens to the vehicle next

  • Turns onto a village road
  • Returns home, is parked out of sight
  • Visits a local mechanic for a quote
  • Moves to a body shop for panel work
  • Is repaired quietly, over days
  • Crosses a taluka or district boundary

The critical evidence does not get destroyed. It simply stops being recorded. That gap is the entire business opportunity.

The business insight

Monitoring every rural road means cameras on every rural road — capital that a district cannot spend and a village will not maintain. But there is one category of place where a damaged vehicle is almost guaranteed to appear: the garage. So TRACEBACK does not monitor roads. It monitors the repair ecosystem.

02 — Who we serve

The victim is the customer. Deliberately.

Not a police department, not an insurer, not a district. The person harmed pays for the search, and that keeps the incentive honest.

Customer

Hit-and-run victim

The person struck, or their immediate family. Frequently a daily-wage or small-trader household — which is precisely why the price point has to be low and the outcome has to be concrete.

User

Victim or assisting relative

Often the son, daughter or neighbour who has the smartphone. The person physically standing at the accident spot taking photographs is usually not the person who fills the form.

Payer

Victim / family — initially

No procurement, no tender, no dependency on a government pilot. Insurance and fleet revenue comes later and subsidises the network; it is never a prerequisite for serving the victim.

One corridor. Not the whole Pune district.

Beachhead: a defined rural/semi-rural Pune cluster

Launch across a district and you have no network, no density and no economics. Launch across a single corridor — a handful of villages, the roads that connect them, and the garages that sit on those roads — and density becomes achievable.

The specific corridor must be selected from data, not chosen because it happens to be scenic rural Pune:

  • Accident frequency and severity mix
  • Road connectivity and vehicle flow
  • Garage and body-shop density
  • Population of the villages served
  • Police-station and jurisdictional boundaries
  • Existing CCTV / ANPR coverage (to avoid duplicating it)

Three layers. The network is the asset, not the model.

The old shape was Victim → AI → Mechanic, which made the AI look essential. The new shape puts the physical network first.

06 — How a lead is found

No GNN. A scoring function you can argue with.

A weighted, explainable score. Every point is attributable to a factor, which means a human reviewer can disagree with it — and that is the point.

What this output is not. A match score is a ranking of attention, not an identification. No candidate is presented as the offending vehicle. A trained reviewer checks photographs, and the outcome is either "worth pursuing" or "not worth pursuing" — nothing more aggressive than that.

Scoring weights used by the MVP

Damage location & characteristics30
Vehicle category25
Colour20
Garage position on probable route15
Arrival time plausibility10
Photographic similarity (phase 2)—
Registration continuity (if known)—

Route and timing are graded, not pass/fail — a garage two hours from the scene on the right road is stronger evidence than one twenty kilometres away on the right road. Colour and category are deliberately low-weight: a lot of rural vehicles are white, and "SUV" is not a rare finding in a corridor. Notice what happens when three identical white SUVs turn up — the engine separates them by timing and route, not appearance, and two of the three drop out of the High band. That is the honest behaviour of a rules engine, and it is exactly the point at which image similarity starts earning its cost.

09 — If ₹499 is not enough

The victim is the front door, not the whole shop.

Hit-and-run investigation is episodic by nature. Low-frequency consumer cases cannot carry the cost of building network density on their own — so higher-value recurring revenue eventually subsides them.

Today

Victim

₹99–₹499 per investigation. Low margin per case, but it is the demand signal that justifies everything else, and it is honest about who pays.

Next

Insurance

Claims investigation, hit-and-run verification, pre-inspection photography, fraud checks. Recurring, and the same infrastructure.

Next

Fleet operators

Incident investigation and repair documentation for commercial vehicles. Fleets generate continuous activity, not one-off cases.

Later

Garages (premium)

Paid tiers of the garage software — inventory, pricing, customer communication — once the free pilot has proven habitual use.

Later

Police / Government

Geospatial + garage-network investigative infrastructure, deployed as a supplementary evidence layer rather than a replacement for police work.

The mismatch to watch. Victim purchases are low-frequency and one-off; network density is a fixed, upfront cost. If each case only arrives every few weeks, the revenue per garage is the constraint — which is another argument for the garage software being free and genuinely used, rather than a thin paid tier nobody renews.
10 — The honest context

Compensation and identification are two different problems.

There is already a government mechanism for hit-and-run compensation. Ignoring that would be both dishonest and commercially stupid.

Existing

Hit-and-run compensation

MoRTH's 2022 scheme provides ₹2 lakh in the case of death and ₹50,000 for grievous injury, subject to the scheme's conditions.

A victim may not know they are entitled to this. That is a separate failure, and it is not the one we solve.

Ministry of Road Transport & Highways →
Ours

Identification & evidence

Identifying the vehicle, establishing what it was carrying at the time, and building a record that is usable in an FIR, a claim, or a civil case.

The compensation scheme pays you regardless of who hit you. Identification determines who is responsible. Different question, different customer, different buyer.

Positioning we will not take: "Pay us because nobody else will help you." It is untrue, it is exploitative, and it invites exactly the hostility that kills consumer trust in this category. The line is "compensation and identification are separate problems — we work on the second one."

12 — The MVP

A 30-day concierge pilot. Software later.

Do not build the platform. Build the answer. A WhatsApp number, thirty garages, one corridor, and a person with a spreadsheet.

Victim interface

A WhatsApp number. The victim sends location, time, vehicle description and photographs. Someone replies in plain Marathi or English. That is the entire product surface.

Garage network

Twenty to thirty garages inside one rural Pune corridor, each with the same simple vehicle photography and inspection workflow.

Investigation backend

A person and a spreadsheet. No GNN, no vision pipeline, no model. Map the routes, list the garages, call them, search the records, write up the leads.

1Map the likely escape routes from the incident point
2List participating garages on those corridors
3Contact those garages directly
4Search their submitted vehicle records
5Identify potential matches, verify by phone

Six weeks of manual work is worth more than six months of platform development — because the only question the manual version answers is the only question that matters: does this produce a useful lead, at a cost somebody will pay?
13 — The first experiment

How to actually test this in 30 days

  1. 01

    Select one rural corridor around Pune

    Using the data criteria: accident frequency, road connectivity, garage density, population, police-station boundaries, vehicle movement, existing surveillance.

  2. 02

    Recruit twenty to thirty garages

    Body shops and general garages. Sign up the ones that already do accident work — they are the ones this matters for, and they are also the ones with the habit of paperwork.

  3. 03

    Hand them a free inspection tool

    Lowest possible friction. If a garage needs a training call, the design is wrong.

  4. 04

    Collect for 30 days

    Vehicle type, colour, photographs, visible damage, timestamp, location. The raw material for every later model.

  5. 05

    Build controlled test cases

    Create Vehicle A — a white Bolero, front-right collision. Then find genuinely similar vehicles in the data — Vehicle B, same colour, same panel, same village. Can the system tell them apart, or does it just say "white SUV" every time?

  6. 06

    Run simulated hit-and-run investigations

    Real cases, plus reconstructed ones. Record what a human investigator did, what they found, and how long it took. That time figure is a direct input to the economics.

The old question

"Can AI predict where a hit-and-run vehicle went?"

The question worth testing

"Can a sufficiently dense network of rural garages create enough post-accident vehicle observations to give victims useful investigative leads, at a cost they are willing to pay?"

The second question is harder, more falsifiable, and far more likely to lead to an actual business. The first one is a research question that could be answered "yes" by a model that still loses money on every case.

19 — The concept, in full

One page, no slides.

Problem

A rural hit-and-run victim may know very little about the offending vehicle, while that vehicle can disappear through local roads and be repaired without leaving any searchable digital trail.

Customer

The hit-and-run victim, or their family, in a rural / semi-rural pocket of Pune, Maharashtra.

Solution

A low-cost investigation service that maps the likely escape area and searches a participating local garage network for vehicles matching the accident's characteristics.

Technology

WhatsApp and a PWA, GPS, digital road mapping, vehicle photography, computer vision, probabilistic matching, and a human investigator. The GNN is a later-stage technology, not an MVP requirement.

Revenue

₹99–₹499 victim-paid investigation packages as an initial hypothesis, with future revenue from insurance, fleet operators and institutional investigation services.

Core advantage

Instead of trying to put surveillance cameras on every rural road, TRACEBACK creates observation points at local garages — the places damaged vehicles naturally appear.

Biggest risk

The offending vehicle may never enter a participating garage — or may enter one without leaving enough distinctive evidence to establish anything useful. No amount of model sophistication fixes that.

First MVP

One rural Pune corridor, 20–30 garages, WhatsApp victim intake, a simple garage inspection tool, and human-assisted investigation for 30 days.

Success condition

Not "does our AI work?" — but "can we generate useful investigative leads cheaply enough that a victim is willing to pay for them?"

If the answer is yes, build the AI.

If the answer is no, then spending ₹10–20 lakh on a sophisticated graph network and computer-vision platform would be solving the wrong problem beautifully.

Build the network first. Prove the investigation economics second. Automate with AI third.
